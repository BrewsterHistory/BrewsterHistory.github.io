The House Called “By Ways”

Henry H. Wells’s Eastview Avenue home — its sun parlor, its $7,000 listing, and its passage through four owners in four years — with a brief life of the family that gave Brewster its bank, its newspaper, and “Mr. Brewster” himself.

Wells residence, by 1913Listed for sale, 1922Four owners, 1922–1926

Draft narrative — a residential thread of the Eastview Avenue series, anchored to the St. Lawrence O’Toole hub. Built on four dated Brewster Standard clippings (1913–1926), the existing project narrative, the 1910 Sanborn appearance reported there, and a modern Village of Brewster property-record card and tax map for the parcel (Map 5 / Block 3 / Lot 2 ≡ tax 67.26‑2‑19), and seventeen conveyances pulled on the lot and its parent block, plus two founding-era farm deeds behind them. Where this now stands: the lot’s full title history is documented — from its creation in 1894 to the present — and its parent block back to Octavia A. Mead’s 1890 purchase. Behind the block, an ancestral farm is documented to a Samuel Brewster–to–Albert Brush deed of 1849 (the source of an iron-ore reservation that propagated down to 1890) — almost certainly the ground the block was carved from, but shown as a separate, older chain until a deed places the block within the farm. That the lot is the Wells family’s “By Ways” is now a very strong identification: the deed-documented ownership runs Wells → Erickson (1922) → Jones (1923) → Reinhardt (1926), matching the Brewster Standard’s account of “By Ways” name-for-name and date-for-date — though no deed itself uses the name “By Ways,” and the 1922 title-holder was Caroline C. Wells (the paper’s named resident being Henry H. Wells). The 1922 Wells–Erickson deed (recited, not yet pulled) and everything before it — toward the subdivision that created the lot — remain to be traced. A separate reservation about the standing building (the 1959 Ganong move; a shared Griffin name) is set out in Section V. Current owners’ names and the property’s street number are withheld by project policy. Pre-1929 newspaper text is quoted directly; later material is paraphrased. Corrections welcome — see Contact.

I. At a glance

“By Ways” was the Eastview Avenue residence of Henry H. Wells — the man who would become, in a centennial headline a half-century later, “Mr. Brewster.” The house carries a proper name in every clipping, the period habit of christening a fine home rather than numbering it, and it appears in the record across a tight thirteen-year window: present by the 1910 Sanborn map, given a sun parlor in 1913, listed for sale in 1922, and then sold, resold, and sold again until 1926.

What makes the property worth a piece of its own is that compression. In the four years from 1922 to 1926 “By Ways” passed through four hands — Henry H. Wells, then Charles Erickson of Mahopac, then a New York City singer named William Jones, then L. A. Reinhardt of the Associated Gas & Electric Company — each transfer reported in the Brewster Standard. The house is also a doorway into the Wells family, whose three generations sit behind a striking share of the village’s institutions and behind the development of Eastview Avenue itself.

One note belongs at the top. The clippings place the house on “Eastview avenue” (and, in 1913, “East View avenue”) but never give a street number, and this piece deliberately does not assign one — the parcel is identified by deed and metes-and-bounds, not by address. Section V sets out that title trail in full: an unbroken chain of deeds now runs back to a 1922 sale by Caroline C. Wells, matching, step for step, the newspaper’s account of how “By Ways” left the Wells family and passed to Erickson, Jones, and Reinhardt.

II. The Wells family — bank, newspaper, and “Mr. Brewster”

The Wells story in Brewster begins with Major Frank Wells (1841–1919). Born in Litchfield, Connecticut, he served as an officer in the 13th Connecticut Volunteers — the siege of Port Hudson and the Shenandoah Valley campaign under Sheridan — and settled in Brewster in 1869 as bookkeeper at Gail Borden’s milk condensery. He boarded with the widow Crosby on Main Street and married her daughter, Caroline Crosby. From the condensery he rose into the institution that would define the family, co-founding the village’s First National Bank (organized as Borden, Wells & Co.) as cashier and later president; for a time he also ran the Brewster Standard, and he commanded the Crosby Post of the Grand Army of the Republic. He died at his residence at the top of Prospect Street on December 17, 1919, in his seventy-ninth year. The cross street at the By Ways corner — Cornell Avenue, recorded in the deeds as “a.k.a. Wells Street” — carries the family name.

Frank Wells is also, separately, a figure behind the physical Eastview Avenue: the project’s narrative has him consolidating open land at the village’s eastern edge around 1903–1913 and selling building lots there — the “sister houses” at 14 and 16 Eastview rose on lots he conveyed to the builder William M. Smalley about 1911. The By Ways lot, though, was not part of that activity. As the deed trail shows (Section V), it was carved out earlier and by a different hand: Octavia A. Mead subdivided her own Prospect Street land — cutting the two “new streets” — and sold this bare corner to Elias B. Brundage in 1894 for $400; Brundage appears to have built the house that year (he resold for $2,500 in 1895). It passed Brundage → Reed → Caroline C. Wells in 1902. So the family bought an existing eight-year-old house on a lot someone else had platted; they neither cut the streets nor raised the house. The lot’s origin lies with Mead and Brundage, not the Wellses.

Henry Hubbard Wells (1877–1978), the youngest of Frank and Caroline’s three children, was the “By Ways” resident the 1913 clipping names. Born in his grandmother Crosby’s Main Street home, schooled at Hotchkiss and Yale (1900), a Spanish-American War veteran, he trained in law and married Caroline Washburn in 1909 — a second Caroline Wells, not to be confused with his mother. By the time his father died in 1919 he was practicing law in White Plains (the obituary calls him “of White Plains”); a 1977 centennial profile describes him returning to Brewster after that death, needed to attend to the family’s affairs, and settling not back on Eastview but into a home remodeled from Samuel Church’s former “Casino” near the Prospect Street house. He went on to be mayor (1935–1947) and Putnam’s long-serving Selective Service director, the “Mr. Brewster” who saw servicemen off at the railroad station; the village middle school carries his name. The “Casino” and “By Ways” were always distinct houses, and the record keeps them apart.

That sequence resolves the one name the deed trail turned on. The 1922 grantor was Caroline C. Wells — and the middle initial is the key: C for Crosby. The seller was Caroline Crosby Wells (1850–1939), Frank’s wife and (by 1922) widow — not Henry’s wife, Caroline Washburn Wells. The 1902 deed sharpens it further: Caroline bought this lot in her own name in 1902, while Frank was still living, and held it for twenty years. So the occupancy-versus-title distinction is unusually clean here — the lot was Caroline’s throughout (1902–1922), while her son Henry was the one living at “By Ways” in 1913; she sold it in 1922 once he had moved on. (Two small uncertainties are worth keeping: the obituaries give Frank and Caroline’s marriage as 1871 in some notices and 1874 in others; and the Crosby line — Caroline’s mother was the widow Pauline Crosby of Main Street — is locally prominent, but any tie to the Enoch or Fanny Crosby of the project’s other markers is unproven.)

III. The house: a sun parlor and a $7,000 listing

The first dated glimpse of the house as the Wells home is a small society note from the winter of 1913 — a carpenter’s improvement worth a paragraph in the Standard:

“By Ways,” the residence of Henry H. Wells on East View avenue, has just received the addition of a handsome sun parlor at the hands of a local carpenter. This should prove a very enjoyable luxury during the winter months.

— Brewster Standard, December 19, 1913

Nine years later the house is described far more fully — in its own for-sale advertisement. It gives the only physical inventory of “By Ways” in the record: nine rooms and a bath, steam heat, village water, and a barn “suitable for garage and extra rooms,” offered at $7,000. That is the profile of a substantial, fully modern home for the early 1920s — central heat and municipal water were not universal — and the barn-to-garage line catches the exact moment the automobile was displacing the horse.

IV. Four owners in four years, 1922–1926

Within days of the listing, the house was sold. On March 3, 1922 the Standard reported that Charles Erickson of Mahopac had bought “By Ways” through the real-estate agent James Murty, for a consideration of $6,500 — five hundred dollars under the asking price — and that Erickson’s daughter and son-in-law, Mr. and Mrs. Joseph Brearton, would “occupy the premises at once.” Title and occupancy already part company here: Erickson bought, the Breartons lived in it.

Erickson held the house only briefly. By the time it changed hands again — reported April 16, 1926 — the owner of record was no longer Erickson but William Jones, described as a “noted singer of New York City,” and the buyer was L. A. Reinhardt, superintendent of the Associated Gas & Electric Company for its Harlem Valley Division. The same item supplies, in one sentence, the chain it had been building all along:

James Murty, real estate agent, has sold “By Ways,” the residence property of William Jones, noted singer of New York City, to L. A. Reinhardt… The property was formerly the home of H. H. Wells, and for a short time was owned by Charles Erickson. During the past year Mr. and Mrs. L. A. Reinhardt and family have lived at “By Ways.”

— Brewster Standard, April 16, 1926

So the reported sequence is Wells → Erickson (March 1922) → Jones → Reinhardt (1926) — with the Reinhardts occupying the house roughly a year before they bought it. This corrects the earlier project narrative, which carried the house straight from Erickson to Reinhardt and left William Jones out entirely. It is worth stating plainly what kind of evidence this is: these are newspaper accounts of ownership and occupancy, valuable and contemporary but not the same as a reviewed deed. The chain is firm enough to publish as a reported chain; promoting it to a documented one means pulling the conveyances, which have not yet been examined.

V. The parcel — tracing the title back to “By Ways”

To carry “By Ways” forward to a surviving lot, we start from a modern Village of Brewster property-record card. It describes a parcel in the Town of Southeast assessment — Map 5 / Block 3 / Lot 2, carried today as tax 67.26‑2‑19 — measuring 60 × 132 feet, with an assessed lot value of $1,500 and a building value of $5,500. On the modern tax map this is the corner lot at the Eastview Avenue frontage, in the same block that holds St. Lawrence O’Toole church and school to the south — which would place “By Ways” at the head of the block whose far end the parish reshaped (the subject of the Ganong and Morehouse pieces).

The card’s ownership column, however, only reaches back to the mid-twentieth century. Its earliest entry is Kenneth & Helen Griffen at Liber 466/337, followed by Helen & Gilmer Griffin (658/25), Louise Dakes (658/27), “Vante & Hobbeland” (716/978, 1974), Stephen Braun (792/61, 1983), Thomas J. & Susan L. Rogers (1005/170, 1988), and the current owners (1481/237, 1999), plus a later utility easement (1817/12). Names of the present owners are withheld by project policy. The $16.50 in revenue stamps on the Griffen deed implies a sale price of about $15,000 at the period rate of $1.10 per $1,000 — the same documentary-stamp arithmetic used in the Ganong piece.

That column is a list of names, not a chain of evidence. To turn it into one, each conveyance has to be pulled and read. Three of those reads — the 1999, 1988, and 1983 deeds — are now done, and together they do more than their late dates suggest.

The 1999 bargain-and-sale deed (Liber 1481 / p. 237; recorded BK 1481 PG 238–239) carries a full Schedule A legal description, and that description does two things at once. It locates the parcel by metes-and-bounds — beginning at the corner where the northwesterly side of Eastview Avenue meets Cornell Avenue, “a.k.a. Wells Street” — and it measures it: 60.00 ft of frontage on Eastview, running back 130.10 ft, a second 60.00 ft line, and 130.10 ft returning to the start. A 60 × 130 ft corner rectangle. The 60.00 ft frontage matches the property-record card and the tax map exactly; the 130.10 ft depth runs about two feet under the card’s approximate 132 ft — close enough to be the same lot, and a discrepancy worth carrying forward as the older deeds are read.

Two details earn their place in the table. First, the abutters: the deed names the adjoining owners as Truran (to the southwest, along Eastview), Lawlor and Howley (to the rear and along the cross street). Those surnames are the handholds for the back-trace — the same names, recurring in an earlier deed’s description, are how a parcel is confirmed across a change of owner. Second, and harder to overlook: the cross street is Wells Street. The family that built this end of Eastview Avenue, and that owned “By Ways,” has its name on the very corner the deed begins from. That is not yet proof — a street name is suggestive, not dispositive — but it is exactly the kind of corroboration the deed trail exists to test.

That thread has now been pulled. The predecessor deed — Liber 1005 / p. 170, dated Feb. 22, 1988 and recorded that April — conveys the parcel from Stephen Braun and Beth Sattinger to the Rogerses, and its description is not merely similar to the 1999 deed but word-for-word identical: the same corner, the same abutters, the same 60.00 and 130.10 ft runs, and the same compass bearings down to the second of arc (S 28° 12′ 33″ W, N 60° 38′ 50″ W, and so on). That is exactly the confirmation the back-trace is built to produce: the lot does not shift or subdivide between 1988 and 1999 — it is one stable surveyed parcel, and the abutter-name method holds. The 1988 deed also adds a name the assessor’s card omitted — Beth Sattinger, co-owner with Braun.

The 1983 deed (Liber 792 / p. 61, July 21) carries the trail back another step and does three useful things. It conveys the parcel from Bjarne Vatne to Stephen Braun and Beth Sattinger, “as tenants in common,” — which settles the small question the 1988 deed had raised: Sattinger was on the title from the start, a co-owner with Braun, not a later addition. It repeats the by-now-familiar metes-and-bounds (a one-minute difference in a single bearing aside), with the abutters given as Truran “formerly Storm,” Lawlor, and Howley — the “formerly Storm” a small bonus, pushing the neighbor’s chain back a generation. And, most usefully, it states a Tax Map Designation in the deed itself: Town of Southeast, Section 5, Block 3, Lot 2 — the exact parcel on the modern property-record card. That is the firmest tie yet between the deed trail and the surveyed lot: not inference from frontage and corner, but the assessor’s own identifier written into a 1983 conveyance.

Two things in this deed reshape the rows above it. First, the property-record card’s garbled “Vante & Hobbeland” clearly sits over the name Bjarne Vatne, the 1983 grantor, who gave his own residence as Eastview Avenue — he lived on the property. Second, a fork: where the card showed a single deed into that owner (716/978), the 1983 deed recites two — “the same premises conveyed to the grantor… by deeds recorded in Libers 716 p. 978 and 732 p. 1003.” Vatne received this corner in two instruments, only one of which the card records; which is which, and why, is what the next pull settles.

One figure is worth banking. The 1988 and 1999 transfers were nominal ($10 and “other valuable consideration”), but the 1983 deed bears a New York State transfer tax of $112.20, which at the period rate of $1.10 per $1,000 implies a sale price of roughly $102,000 — the first real price in the recent chain, and a marker to compare against whatever the older deeds reveal. (The price is inferred from the tax stamp, not stated in the deed.)

Pulling the first of the two source deeds — Liber 716 / p. 978, dated December 27, 1973 — resolves the fork and the garbled name in one stroke, and turns out to be the most informative document on the trail so far. It conveys the parcel from Louise Davis to Bjarne Vatne and Johannes Hobbesland, together. So the card’s “Vante & Hobbeland” was never one name garbled — it was two real buyers, Vatne and Hobbesland, flattened onto a single line. And the card’s prior owner “Louise Dakes” is Louise Davis, this deed’s grantor: the assessor’s “Dakes” is a misread “Davis.” Two card errors corrected by one document.

That also unforks the two-deed puzzle. Vatne and Hobbesland bought the corner jointly in 1973; the second deed the 1983 conveyance recited, Liber 732/1003, is therefore almost certainly the instrument by which Vatne later acquired Hobbesland’s share to become sole owner — not a second parcel, but the buyout of a co-tenant’s half-interest. (That stays an inference until 732/1003 is read.) Neither 1973 buyer lived on Eastview Avenue then — Vatne gave a Mahopac address, Hobbesland a New Jersey one — yet by 1983 Vatne’s deed places him on the street itself. Title and residence begin apart and then converge: exactly the occupancy-versus-ownership distinction this project keeps in view.

Most valuable of all, the 1973 deed carries two descriptions of the same lot. One is the modern survey already seen — the Eastview/Cornell corner, 60.00 × 130.10, Truran–Lawlor–Howley. The other is older, and reads like the parcel’s birth certificate: it begins at a corner “adjoining a new street running east and west,” 132 feet from land “now or formerly owned by Octavia A. Mead, which fronts on Prospect Street,” runs 132 feet to “a new street running parallel with Prospect Street,” then 60 feet along it to “lands now or formerly owned by Harriet Storm.” Those “new streets” are Eastview Avenue and Cornell/Wells Street when they had just been cut — the development era itself — and the lot is fixed by reference to Prospect Street, the Mead land, and the Storm land rather than to the later neighbors. This is the bridge the back-trace needs: it couples the modern survey to the language a pre-survey deed would use, and hands us two fresh reference names — Mead and Storm — to follow toward the 1920s.

It settles a small loose end too. The older description gives the depth as a round 132 feet — the very figure the assessor’s card still carries — while the survey refines it to 130.10. The two-foot gap noted earlier is simply the distance between a nominal early dimension and a later instrument survey, not a sign of two different lots.

The next two deeds were filed back to back, and together they carry the trail across the 1960s and name the owner the whole chain has been climbing toward. Liber 658 / p. 25 (July 22, 1966) is a gift — “no stamps required, transferred by gift” — from W. Kenneth and Helen G. Griffin into a family trust of their own making, with William K. Griffin (the same man: “W. Kenneth” is “William K.”) and Walter Pilner as trustees. Liber 658 / p. 27 (Nov. 11, 1967) is the trust’s answering gift: the trustees — now Helen Griffin, George Thompson, Thomas DePaoli, Sol Fleck, and Pilner — convey the parcel to Louise Davis for one dollar. So the property passed Griffin → trust → Davis entirely by gift, and Davis — who would sell to Vatne and Hobbesland in 1973 — received the corner rather than bought it, a hint of a family or personal tie worth chasing (she and the Griffins both turn up on Elmhurst Avenue).

The decisive line is inside the 1966 deed. It recites: “the same premises conveyed to the party of the first part by Thomas Johnson, by deed dated Oct. 5, 1955… in Liber 466 of Deeds at page 337.” That is the card’s earliest entry, and it now has parties and a date: Thomas Johnson sold this corner to the Griffins in 1955. Johnson is a new name, sitting just below the long-standing gap — and 466/337, when pulled, should recite his source in turn, the next step toward Reinhardt’s 1926 purchase of “By Ways.” The gap has gone from “c. 1926–1950s, unknown” to a single bounded stretch: 1926 (Reinhardt) up to 1955 (Johnson → Griffin).

Two more notes from this pair. The assessor’s “Helen & Gilmer Griffin” for 658/25 is another garble — the grantees were William K. Griffin and Walter Pilner as trustees, not a “Gilmer.” And the trusteeship itself shifted between 1966 and 1967: William K. Griffin, a trustee in July 1966, is absent from the November 1967 conveyance, replaced by Helen and three others — the kind of change that usually marks a death or resignation, and a small thread in its own right. Both 1966 and 1967 deeds still use the old 132-foot, “new streets,” Mead-and-Storm description; the modern survey does not appear until the 1973 sale, so 466/337 and anything older should speak the same older language.

One quiet thread runs under all of this. The Griffins gave their residence as Elmhurst Avenue, not Eastview — they held title to this corner from 1955 without living on it — and Davis took the gift while living in Holmes. Through the 1955–1973 stretch, in other words, the people on the title were mostly not the people in the house. Who actually occupied “By Ways” in those years is a separate question from who owned it — the occupancy-versus-title distinction again — and one the deeds alone will not answer.

Which brings us to the deed the whole trail was climbing toward. Liber 466 / p. 337 — a warranty deed with full covenants, dated October 5, 1955 — conveys the corner from Thomas Johnson to W. Kenneth and Helen Griffin, as tenants by the entirety, with the same old 132-foot description and the same “buildings and improvements thereon.” It is the earliest entry on the assessor’s card, and reaching a 1955 warranty deed that still carries the development-era description is itself a result: the parcel and its building run continuously from at least 1955 forward, and the language matches what an older deed would use.

But it does not do the one thing we hoped. Unlike the 1999 and 1966 deeds, the 1955 deed carries no “same premises” recital — it never names where Thomas Johnson got the property. The trail cannot ride a recital across the last stretch of the gap; from here it has to be picked up the harder way, by searching the grantee index for Thomas Johnson to find when, and from whom, he acquired the corner somewhere between 1926 and 1955. That is the next move at the Clerk’s office — not a deed the record points to, but a name to chase.

Two smaller things the deed does give. Johnson’s residence is recorded as Tilly Foster — the hamlet on the far side of the town, home of the old Tilly Foster mine — so he too held this corner without living on it, one more non-resident owner in a row of them. And the gap is now bounded at both ends by a name: Reinhardt buying “By Ways” in 1926, Johnson selling it in 1955. What stands between them is a single question — how the corner passed from the Reinhardt era into Johnson’s hands — and the abutter and reference names the older deeds preserve (Truran/Storm, Lawlor, Howley, Mead) are the tools for matching it when it surfaces.

The grantee-index search for Thomas Johnson found it. Liber 330 / p. 242, a warranty deed of April 21, 1947, conveys the corner from Robert J. and Madelon D. Alcorn to Thomas Johnson, again with the old 132-foot description and the buildings on it. The federal documentary stamps total $13.75, which at the 1940s rate of $1.10 per $1,000 implies a price of about $12,500 — a real figure (the stated $100 is nominal), and a touch under the ~$15,000 Johnson would get from the Griffins eight years later. And this deed, unlike Johnson’s sale, carries a recital: it conveys “the same premises conveyed to the [Alcorns] by Edward E. and Edith G. Heinen… by deed dated April 23, 1946… in Liber 307 of deeds at page 245.” The trail rides a recital again — we have a liber and page to walk to, not just a name to chase.

The dates are worth noting. The Alcorns bought from the Heinens in April 1946 and sold to Johnson in April 1947 — they held the corner barely a year, and gave their own address as Oak Street, not Eastview: non-resident owners, like nearly everyone on this stretch of the chain. So the back-trace now reads Heinen → Alcorn (1946) → Johnson (1947) → Griffin (1955), and the gap has shrunk again: from Reinhardt’s 1926 purchase up to the Heinens, who already held the corner by 1946. The next pull, 307/245, should name the Heinens’ source — and that is the deed that may finally reach the Reinhardt era.

It does two notable things. First, after a long run of owners who lived elsewhere — Tilly Foster, Oak Street, Elmhurst Avenue, Holmes, Mahopac — the 1946 deed (Liber 307 / p. 245) finally gives a grantor who lived on the property: Edward E. and Edith G. Heinen, who recorded their own residence as this very corner. They are the first owner-occupants the back-trace has reached — for the Heinen years, title and occupancy coincide — and the deed incidentally states the street number the project otherwise withholds, confirming it from a primary source even as the page keeps it private.

Second, the recital carries the trail past the war years: the Heinens took the premises “from J. Raymond and Elizabeth B. Ramsey… by deed dated March 9, 1939… in Liber 239 of deeds at page 262.” So the chain now reaches 1939, and the Ramseys are the next name back. The gap has narrowed to the thirteen years between Reinhardt’s 1926 purchase and the Ramseys’ ownership — and 239/262, the next pull, should recite the Ramseys’ own source. One or two more steps of the same kind should reach the Reinhardt era and, with it, the documented history of “By Ways.”

That next deed delivers the most suggestive find of the whole back-trace. Liber 239 / p. 262 (March 9, 1939) conveys the corner from the Ramseys — who held it barely five months — to the Heinens, “subject to… a bond and mortgage in the sum of $4,950… held by the Putnam County Savings Bank,” which the Heinens assumed. With $7.00 in documentary stamps on the equity above that mortgage, the 1939 value works out to roughly $11,000 — sitting neatly below the later ~$12,500 (1947) and ~$15,000 (1955). But the line that matters is the recital: the Ramseys had taken the premises “from New York State Electric & Gas Corporation… by deed dated October 17, 1938… in Liber 237 of deeds at page 442.”

The corner had been in the hands of a gas-and-electric utility. That is exactly the kind of owner the clippings would lead one to expect here: the man who bought “By Ways” in 1926, L. A. Reinhardt, was superintendent of the Associated Gas & Electric Company for its Harlem Valley Division — and Associated Gas & Electric was the holding system that controlled New York State Electric & Gas. A utility-executive buyer in 1926, a utility corporation holding the lot until 1938: the two ends of the gap are reaching toward each other. It is not yet proof — the deed that would join them, 237/442 (NYSE&G’s own source), has not been read — but for the first time the documentary chain and the newspaper record are pointing at the same kind of owner. The next pull is the one that may close the identification of “By Ways.”

It reaches the founding sale. Liber 237 / p. 442 — New York State Electric & Gas’s 1938 deed to the Ramseys, signed by the company’s president — does not stop at one recital. It sets out the entire chain of title behind it, four conveyances deep, all the way to 1926:

“BEING the same premises conveyed by William R. Jones to Mabel C. Reinhardt by deed dated May 3, 1926… in Liber 135… page 362. ALSO being the same premises conveyed by Mabel C. Reinhardt to C M-M Corporation… 1928… ALSO… by C M M Corporation to the Associated Real Properties, Inc.… Also… by the Associated Real Properties, Inc. to New York State Electric & Gas Corporation… 1936.”

— recital in Liber 237/442, October 1938

The first link in that recital is the join the whole back-trace was built to find. William R. Jones to Mabel C. Reinhardt, May 3, 1926 — the same year, parties, and buyer’s household as the sale the Brewster Standard reported, when James Murty sold “By Ways,” the property of the singer William Jones, to Reinhardt. (The paper named the husband, L. A. Reinhardt, the Associated Gas & Electric superintendent; the deed shows title taken in his wife Mabel’s name — an ordinary arrangement.) This is where the deed record and the newspaper record meet. It is worth being exact about what that match does and does not establish. The continuous title proves this lot was Jones’s and passed to Reinhardt in 1926; the newspaper proves a house called “By Ways” was Jones’s and passed to Reinhardt in 1926. That the two are the same property is a strong circumstantial inference — surname, surname, and year all agree, and the geography, dimensions, and “Wells Street” corner all fit — but no deed in the chain uses the words “By Ways” or names Henry H. Wells. The identification is as firm as the documentary record allows short of a deed that says so, and it should be read as that, not as a deed-proven fact.

The middle of that recital also explains the utility ownership that first hinted at the answer. Mabel Reinhardt sold the corner in 1928 not to a neighbor but to the C M-M Corporation, which passed it to Associated Real Properties, Inc. — the real-estate arm of the Associated Gas & Electric system her husband worked for — which in turn handed it to New York State Electric & Gas in 1936. The house Reinhardt bought in 1926 was absorbed into his employer’s corporate holdings and only released to a private buyer (the Ramseys) in 1938. The mortgage tells the same story from another angle: Mabel Reinhardt’s $5,500 loan from the Putnam County Savings Bank rode along under the corporate owners, was assumed by the Ramseys in 1938, and had been paid down to $4,950 by the time the Heinens took it on in 1939.

That 1926 deed has now itself been pulled — Liber 135/362 — which does two things and disappoints in a third. It confirms the recital exactly (Jones to Mabel C. Reinhardt, May 3, 1926, this lot), so the founding conveyance is a deed in hand rather than a summary. And it corroborates the singer: Jones gave his residence as West 111th Street in New York City and signed as “unmarried,” squarely consistent with the Standard’s “noted singer of New York City.” But it carries no source recital — like Johnson’s 1955 deed, it never says where Jones got the property. So at that point the deed record stopped, at its far end, with Jones in 1926, and the only way further back was a grantee-index search for Jones’s own purchase.

That search found it — and it broke the question open. Book 126 / p. 277, a full-covenant deed of August 1, 1923, conveys this lot from Charles J. and Hannah M. Erickson to William R. Jones for a real $7,000 — the same figure By Ways had been advertised at in 1922, and just above the $6,500 Erickson reportedly paid for it. More important, this deed does carry a recital, and the name in it is the one the whole trace was reaching for: “the same premises as were conveyed by Caroline C. Wells to Charles J. Erickson, by deed dated March 13, 1922… in Book 122… page 525.” A Wells held the title to this lot, and sold it to Erickson in 1922 — exactly the transfer the Standard reported when “By Ways” left the Wells family.

That changes the strength of the identification. It no longer rests on a single 1926 coincidence: the deed-documented ownership now runs Wells → Erickson (1922) → Jones (1923) → Reinhardt (1926), and the newspaper record gives the very same four names in the very same order on the very same dates for a house called “By Ways.” A four-step match of parties and years across two independent records is about as firm as an identification gets without the name itself appearing on an instrument — which it still does not. One nuance the deed raises, this project resolves rather than leaves open (Section II): the grantor is Caroline C. Wells, while the resident the 1913 paper named was Henry H. Wells. The family history settles it — Caroline Crosby Wells was Henry’s mother, and she held this lot in her own name; Henry was the son who lived there.

And the next pull went deeper still — not Caroline’s sale but her purchase. Book 90 / p. 69, a full-covenant deed of November 10, 1902, conveys this lot from Deborah H. Reed of Mount Vernon to Caroline C. Wells of Brewster for $2,900 — so Caroline did not inherit the corner or receive it from Frank; she bought it herself, in 1902, and held it for twenty years before selling to Erickson. A small confirming detail sits on the page: the notary is Frederic S. Barnum, the White Plains attorney who would later be Henry H. Wells’s law partner — the family’s lawyer, handling the family’s purchase.

This corrects something a reasonable guess had gotten wrong. Because Frank Wells is known to have developed other Eastview land around 1903–1913, it was tempting to assume the By Ways lot was a Wells creation. It was not. The 1902 deed describes the “new streets” as already cut and recites the lot’s prior passage — “the same premises… conveyed by Elias B. Brundage to Deborah H. Reed… January 16, 1895… Book 76… page 525.” The Wellses bought an existing platted lot; its origin lies further back, with Brundage and whoever first laid the streets out. The next pull, then, is Book 76/525 (Brundage–Reed, 1895), which should recite Brundage’s own source — and behind it, the Clerk’s filed map records, where a subdivision of the Prospect Street farm into these lots and streets should finally appear.

That pull lands almost on the origin. Book 76/525, a warranty deed of January 16, 1895, conveys the lot from Elias B. Brundage to Deborah H. Reed for $2,500 (subject to a $1,300 mortgage Brundage had given Hannah E. Bowen the year before). Its recital names the source, and the name is one that has been sitting in every description in this chain without anyone reading it as an owner: “the same lot conveyed by Octavia A. Mead to Elias B. Brundage… February 3, 1894… book 75… page 474.” Every deed back to 1895 fixes this corner by measuring 132 feet from “the lot of Octavia A. Mead which fronts on Prospect Street.” Mead was not just the neighbour to the west — she was the source. The corner was carved from her Prospect Street land, the two “new streets” cut through it, and she sold this lot to Brundage in February 1894 while keeping her own street-front parcel. That is the lot’s birth as a distinct parcel.

So the full arc of the corner now reads, in deeds: carved from Octavia Mead’s land and sold to Brundage (1894) → Reed (1895) → Caroline Wells (1902) → Erickson (1922) → Jones (1923) → Reinhardt (1926) → the corporate utility owners → the Ramseys (1938) and on through the line of families to today. One pull remains to reach the creation itself — Book 75/474, Mead to Brundage — which should also recite how Octavia Mead held the parent tract, the doorway from “lot” back to “farm,” and ultimately to the filed map that laid the streets out.

It reaches it. Book 75/474, a warranty deed of February 3, 1894, is where this lot begins: Octavia A. Mead conveys the corner to Elias B. Brundage for $400. The description carves it straight out of her own land — the anchor lot is “the lot of party of first part which fronts on Prospect Street,” i.e. Mead’s — and she kept a clause letting her repurchase the lot for its price plus any improvements. So the picture is settled: Mead held a Prospect Street parcel, the two “new streets” were cut through it, and she sold off this corner, retaining her street frontage and a hold on the lot’s future. (The same Frederic S. Barnum notarized it — the conveyancer running through this whole neighborhood.)

The price tells a second story. Brundage paid $400 for the bare lot in February 1894 and sold it for $2,500 eleven months later — a sixfold jump that is hard to read as anything but a house built on the lot during 1894, almost certainly by Brundage. The deeds describe only a “lot of land” well into the 1900s, so this is an inference from the price and the 1894 deed’s talk of “improvements,” not an explicit record — but it would place the building of the house that became “By Ways” in 1894, eight years before the Wells family bought it and nearly twenty before the 1913 sun-parlor notice. What Book 75/474 does not give is Mead’s own source: the deed simply warrants her title. Tracing the land before 1894 therefore leaves this lot behind and becomes a search for Octavia Mead’s acquisition of the parent farm — a grantee-index search, and the start of a different, older chain that runs back through the 19th-century Prospect Street farms toward the patent-era deep horizon.

That search lands too. Book 71/555, a warranty deed of July 28, 1890, conveys to Octavia A. Mead, for $800, not a lot but the whole block: a parcel 264 feet deep and 60 wide, bounded west by Prospect Street, south by Harriet Storm, and north and east by two streets the deed calls only “proposed” — the east-west one “forty-five feet wide.” Those are the future Eastview Avenue and the parallel street that became Cornell / Wells. The arithmetic closes the picture exactly: Mead bought 264 feet of depth in 1890, and in 1894 she sold the eastern 132 to Brundage (this lot) while keeping the western 132 fronting Prospect — the very “lot of Octavia A. Mead which fronts on Prospect Street” that anchors every later description. The streets were “proposed” in 1890 and “new” by 1894, dating the subdivision to those four years.

The grantors point the way deeper. The 1890 sellers were Jacob H. Brush (of Sonoma County, California), Avery S. Brush (of Mitchell County, Iowa), and Sarah E. Van Scoy (of Putnam) — a family scattered across the country, the usual signature of heirs dividing an inheritance. And the deed reserves the iron and mineral rights “as reserved in the deed of Samuel Brewster and wife to Albert Brush.” So behind the Brush family stands a conveyance from a Brewster — of the family that founded and named the village and worked its iron mines — to Albert Brush, presumably these grantors’ forebear. That reservation is the thread into the deep horizon: an index search for the Brush (Albert Brush) holding and the Samuel Brewster deed behind it would carry this ground out of the village lots and back into the founding-era farms, toward the Philipse Patent partition that underlies every parcel in the town. That older chain is a separate undertaking from the lot itself, which is now documented end to end.

Two founding-era deeds have since surfaced, and they reward the caution. The first is the source the 1890 reservation pointed to: a deed of August 15, 1849, by which Samuel Brewster and his wife Eliza L. conveyed to Albert Brush a roughly hundred-acre Southeast farm near Brewster Station, for $6,825 — and in it the Brewsters “reserve to themselves the exclusive right to all iron ore… now or hereafter… found,” with rights to sink shafts and work it. That is the same reservation, almost word for word, that the 1890 deed carried forward forty-one years later; the ore clause is a fingerprint, and it ties the village block, through the Brush family, back to this farm. The date places it precisely at the village’s birth — the Harlem Railroad had just reached Brewster, and the deed’s bounds run along “the line of the ore vein,” the rail road, the Croton River, and Bailey’s garden. (A companion deed of 1851 runs the other way — Albert Brush quitclaiming two small parcels back to Brewster — useful context on their dealings, but not a link in our lot’s descending chain.)

What these do not do is close the splice, and the page does not pretend otherwise. The 1849 deed conveys a hundred-acre farm in chains and links against stone walls and trees; it shows no Prospect Street, no village grid, none of the anchors of our 264 × 60 block, and the chain from Albert Brush’s 1849 purchase down to his heirs’ 1890 sale is unfilled. So the honest picture is two-tier: the lot, documented continuously from its 1894 creation to the present; and behind it an ancestral farm, documented to 1849 and almost certainly the ground the block was later carved from — linked by the reserved-ore clause and the family names, but not yet by a deed that puts the block inside the farm. Confirming that splice, and Samuel Brewster’s own source behind 1849, would carry the story the rest of the way back toward the Philipse Patent.

  • March 3, 1922 — Charles Erickson of Mahopac buys “By Ways” (advertised by Henry H. Wells) through agent James Murty for $6,500; Mr. and Mrs. Joseph Brearton, the purchaser’s daughter and son-in-law, to occupy at once.
  • April 16, 1926 — James Murty sells “By Ways,” then the property of William Jones, “noted singer of New York City,” to L. A. Reinhardt of the Associated Gas & Electric Co. (Harlem Valley Division); recites the prior Wells and Erickson ownership and a year of Reinhardt occupancy.

Methodological note
  • The 1922–1926 ownership chain is established from contemporary newspaper reports; occupancy and title are tracked separately (the Breartons and then the Reinhardts occupied as others held title). The identification of the modern parcel as “By Ways” is established by a continuous chain of deeds running from the present owners back to the documented 1926 sale; the one remaining reservation concerns the standing building (the 1959 Ganong move and a shared Griffin name), treated as a caution rather than a connection.

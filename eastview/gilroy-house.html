The Gilroy House

An Irish immigrant family, the Eastview Avenue home remembered as the village’s first Catholic worship site, and the disputed 1871 land transaction behind the founding of St. Lawrence O’Toole.

Home lot, dwelling by 1863St. Lawrence O’Toole, 1871Mary Gilroy, d. 1914

Draft narrative — built on five physically reviewed Putnam County deeds (Liber 38/417, 44/543, 50/455, 65/567, and the 1851 neighbor deed at X/120), a grantor/grantee index search, Mary Gilroy’s January 1914 Brewster Standard funeral notice, and the Rev. Michael J. Henry obituary of October 6, 1922 (which prints a summary of St. Lawrence O’Toole’s early history drawn from the parish’s own baptismal records). The parish’s 150th-anniversary bulletin history (Tina Grant, June 13, 2021, with corroborating installments June 20 and November 28, 2021) supplies the family’s origin story; it is a modern devotional retelling and is cited as such. Each claim below is graded by source. The central land-transfer claim — that the Gilroys gave the parish its first church property — is not supported by the recorded deed chain and is flagged accordingly. Corrections welcome — see Contact.

I. At a glance

John Gilroy and his wife Mary were Irish immigrants who settled in Brewster around the end of the 1850s and built a household at the north end of what would become Eastview Avenue. The deed record confirms John Gilroy as the owner of that home lot from 1867, on a parcel that already carried a dwelling house as early as 1863. By long parish tradition, the Gilroy home was the place where Brewster’s scattered Catholics gathered for Mass before there was any church — and the family is remembered as the founders behind St. Lawrence O’Toole, the parish established in 1871.

That founding role is firmly attested by a primary source: at Mary Gilroy’s funeral in 1914, the pastor publicly credited the couple with the parish’s establishment, and a memorial tablet to John Gilroy was placed in the new stone church then under construction. What is not firmly attested — despite a century of parish accounts saying so — is the specific mechanism. John Gilroy did buy a lot on Prospect Street in 1871, the lot on which the first church stood; but no recorded deed conveying that lot from Gilroy to the church has been found. Whether the property was a gift, a sale at cost, a conveyance held in trust, or simply land the parish used without a recorded transfer remains, on the documentary record, an open question. This piece keeps the documented facts and the parish tradition carefully separate.

II. The Irish in Brewster, before 1871

The Catholic community John and Mary Gilroy joined was a product of the railroad. The Harlem Railroad reached the area in 1848, and Gail Borden’s condensed-milk factory followed in 1861; together they drew a workforce that included a substantial number of Irish immigrants. The parish’s own history places the wider movement in context: St. Joseph’s in Croton Falls had been founded in 1845 “as part of the Catholic expansion out from the city which followed the Harlem railroad line northwards,” and St. Lawrence O’Toole began in 1871 as a mission of that Croton Falls parish.

Before 1871, Brewster had no Catholic church of its own; the faithful traveled to Carmel or Croton Falls for Mass. The 1922 parish history, drawn from St. Lawrence’s baptismal records, preserves the earliest missionary chain — Jesuit fathers from Fordham College attended the territory first, followed by Rev. Charles Slevin (who took charge from Mt. Kisco to Millerton in 1859) and Rev. John Orsenigo (from 1865) — before the events of 1871 gave Brewster Catholics a building of their own. It is into this gap, in the decade before the parish existed, that the parish tradition places the Gilroy home.

III. The Gilroys at home on “New Street”

According to the parish’s 150th-anniversary history, John and Mary Gilroy were both born in Ireland — John in 1839, Mary in 1840 — arrived in America in 1858, settled in Brewster, where John worked at the railroad depot, and raised thirteen children. These origin details rest on that single 2021 account and should be read as parish tradition rather than verified record. One of them can be tested against a primary source and does not hold: Mary’s 1914 funeral notice gives her age at death as sixty-eight, which places her birth around 1845–46, not 1840. The contemporaneous obituary outranks the modern retelling, so her birth year is best carried as c. 1845–46. The same 1914 notice names two of the daughters — Mrs. James Dwyer of Brewster and Mrs. Thomas Dougherty of Danbury — the only Gilroy children documented here by a primary source.

The family home, by contrast, is documented in detail. The lot sat on the east side of a “contemplated new road” — the future Eastview Avenue, which early clippings called “New Street” — near the old highway that ran past Harvey Bailey’s dwelling, bounded by the lands of Albert Brush to the north and the Ganung family (formerly Samuel Brewster) to the south. Three deeds trace its ownership:

In 1863, Samuel Brewster and his wife Eliza quit-claimed the parcel — a rectangle of about 12,000 square feet (200 by 60 feet) “on which is a dwelling house” — to James C. Gulick for $50 (Liber 38/417). The house, in other words, already stood on the lot eight years before the parish was founded. In 1867, Gulick conveyed the same lot to John Gilroy of the Town of Southeast by warranty deed for $1,000 (Liber 44/543) — the transaction by which the Gilroys became owners of their Eastview home.

That John Gilroy was still living on this lot decades later is fixed by a third deed. In 1890, he sold a sixty-foot strip off the side of his property to Elias B. Brundage for $1,200, reserving a right of way; the parcel is described as running to a line “twenty feet west of the inside stoning of a well now on the premises of John Gilroy” (Liber 65/567). The phrasing places Gilroy in residence on the home lot in 1890 and confirms that the 1863/1867 parcel was indeed his homestead — the residence the parish accounts describe as the pre-1871 Mass site.

What the home witnessed before 1871 rests on parish memory rather than record. The 150th-anniversary history states that the Gilroys opened their Eastview home for Masses and baptisms and kept the Stations of the Cross in their living room; a companion installment describes the parish’s founding faith as having lived “in the hearts of the people who prayed in the home of the Gilroys.” These are consistent, repeated parish claims, and they are entirely plausible given that the house demonstrably existed by 1863 — but they are single-author secondary statements, not independently documented, and are carried here as tradition.

IV. The 1871 Prospect Street lot — what the record shows

The central claim in every telling of the Gilroy story is that the family gave St. Lawrence O’Toole its first church property. The deed record both confirms part of this and unsettles the rest.

What is confirmed: on August 12, 1871, John Gilroy purchased a lot in the Village of Brewster from Mary C. Eggleston of Mott Haven for $1,325, by warranty deed recorded ten days later (Liber 50/455). The parcel lay on the east side of Prospect Street Extension, 180 feet north of the Augustus lot, measuring roughly 132 by 60 feet — about 8,000 square feet. This is a separate property from the Eastview home lot: different grantor, different street, different size, four years apart.

The earliest detailed account of what happened next is the parish history printed in 1922, within the Brewster Standard obituary of Rev. Michael J. Henry — a summary the paper attributes to St. Lawrence’s own baptismal records, and the source closest in time to the events:

In 1871 he [Rev. Lawrence McKenna] purchased a lot on Prospect street and an unfinished house, John Gilroy being the supposed purchaser. This good Catholic pioneer transferred the property to the church corporation and the house was remodelled into a church. This edifice was the place of worship for Brewster Catholics for many years.

— Early history of St. Lawrence O’Toole, in the obituary of Rev. Michael J. Henry, Brewster Standard, October 6, 1922

This 1922 framing is notably different from the modern one. In the parish’s 2021 anniversary history, the Gilroys simply owned Prospect Street property and donated it. In the 1922 account, the actual buyer was the pastor, Rev. McKenna, with Gilroy as the “supposed purchaser” — period usage for the ostensible or of-record buyer — who then transferred the property to the church corporation. That reads less like an outright gift of long-held family land and more like a trusted layman taking title to a purchase the priest arranged. The deed adds a further wrinkle: the 1871 instrument conveys a lot, with the standard appurtenances language, and does not in its granting clause name the “unfinished house” the 1922 account describes — so whether the house came with the lot, in a separate instrument, or is a later recollection is itself unsettled.

Open Research — the missing conveyance

No recorded deed from John Gilroy to the church corporation has been located. A search of the Putnam County grantor index turns up Gilroy’s purchase of the Prospect Street lot in 1871, and several later Gilroy conveyances to private parties, but no transfer of that lot to St. Lawrence O’Toole, to a pastor as trustee, or to the diocese. Both the 1922 and 2021 parish accounts state that such a transfer occurred; the title record, as searched, does not yet bear it out.

The possibilities are distinct and consequential. The conveyance may have been recorded under a different grantor (McKenna individually, or a trustee); it may have been a non-deed transfer (a will or long lease); it may simply never have been recorded, as was not unusual for a small parish operating informally in 1871; or the parish may have occupied the property without ever holding recorded title — in which case the long-repeated “they gave us the land” tradition would be a compression of a more complicated reality. This is the same occupancy-versus-title distinction that recurs across this research: a family can be central to a place’s history without the deed record matching the story told about them. Until the outbound instrument (or its confirmed absence) is established, the most the record supports is that Gilroy bought the lot in 1871 and the first church was established on it.

V. St. Lawrence O’Toole afterward

Whatever the mechanism, the first St. Lawrence O’Toole church was, per the 1922 history, a remodeled unfinished house on the Prospect Street lot — not a purpose-built chapel — and it served Brewster’s Catholics for many years. The same source preserves the parish’s nineteenth-century pastoral succession: Rev. Lawrence McKenna (who also bought the parish cemetery); Rev. Patrick Daly, whose brief pastorate ended after he was thrown from his horse; Rev. Patrick J. Healy (from 1877, who moved the parish headquarters from Croton Falls to Brewster and in 1878 bought the rectory from Walter Weed); Rev. Michael J. Henry (from 1889); and Rev. Patrick J. Clancy.

One detail in that succession is worth flagging against the modern account. The 1922 history credits the purchase of the parish’s Eastview Avenue lands to Fr. Henry — “he built an addition to the church, bought the lands on East View avenue and paid the mortgage on the church” — not to the Gilroys. The 2021 bulletin, by contrast, lists Eastview Avenue among properties the Gilroys themselves owned. Both can be partly true: Gilroy held his home lot on the future Eastview from 1867, while the parish’s Eastview land was bought later by Fr. Henry. The two should not be conflated.

By the second decade of the twentieth century the parish had outgrown the remodeled house. The original wooden church remained in use until June 1915, when a new stone church — designed in English Gothic by the architect Robert J. Reiley, its granite hauled from quarries on Marvin Hill — replaced it on the Prospect Street ground; the older wooden building was moved onto the Eastview Avenue property the parish then owned, where it would later be remodeled into a parish school. The stone church’s cornerstone was laid in October 1914, and it stands today at 31 Prospect Street — the same building visible, labeled “St. Lawrence R.C. Church,” on the Sanborn map reproduced in the Morehouse factory piece in this series, just northeast of the old sash-and-blind plant.

VI. After Gilroy — and a word on the name

John Gilroy was still alive and holding property in 1890, when he sold the strip to Brundage; the parish history gives his death as 1898, a date not yet confirmed against a primary source but consistent with the record, since Mary’s 1914 notice describes her as “widow of the late John Gilroy.” Mary herself died in January 1914, age sixty-eight. Her funeral was held at St. Lawrence’s church and from the home of her daughter, Mrs. James Dwyer; the Brewster Standard called it one of the largest the village had known. The pastor, Rev. Thomas Phelan, delivered the tribute that stands as the strongest primary attestation of what the Gilroys had done:

The Catholic church of Brewster loses its oldest and one of its most highly respected pioneers in the death of Mrs. Gilroy… It was largely due, he said, to these faithful souls that the little church of St. Lawrence O’Toole finally became established. The present church building is about to be replaced by a much more substantial edifice of stone, in which a tablet to the memory of the late John Gilroy will be placed.

— “Funeral of Mrs. Mary Gilroy,” Brewster Standard, January 1914

The same notice carries a small but telling correction — the kind of detail that argues for trusting it. The paper had reported the previous week that John Gilroy was once the church’s janitor; it now set the record straight: “While he was one of the pioneers and always active for the welfare of the parish, he was never the regular janitor.” A source willing to correct its own earlier error about a man is a source worth weighting heavily on the things it does affirm.

One caution belongs with the name itself. There was more than one adult John Gilroy in this small community, and the sources tangle them. A separate obituary survives for a John Gilroy who was proprietor of the Putnam House — born in Ireland, arrived 1858, survived by his widow and eight children (sons Edward, Frank, James, and William; daughters Mary, Kate, Margaret, and Helen), with Rev. P. J. Clancy officiating at his funeral. That man cannot be the pioneer: the pioneer John predeceased Mary, whereas this John was survived by his wife, and the child count and other details diverge. The deed index reinforces the multiplicity — an 1893 conveyance runs from Hugh Gilroy to John Gilroy — so any “John Gilroy” entry has to be matched to the right man by date and spouse before it is used. The Putnam House obituary is set aside here as belonging, in all likelihood, to a second and younger John Gilroy, pending confirmation of the relationship.

Open Research

The outbound church conveyance. The single most important open item: locating the instrument (or confirming the absence) by which the 1871 Prospect Street lot passed from Gilroy to the parish. The grantee index should be checked under the church, the diocese, and McKenna-as-trustee for 1871–1873.

The third Gilroy property. The 2021 parish history states the Gilroys owned property on Eastview Avenue, Prospect Street, and Main Street. The Eastview (home) and Prospect (church) parcels are now documented by deed; the Main Street parcel has not yet been pulled.

Mary’s birth year. The 1914 obituary (age 68) points to c. 1845–46; the 2021 parish history says 1840. Carried here per the contemporaneous source, with the conflict noted. No birth or baptismal record has been located for either spouse.

John’s death. The 1898 death year rests solely on the 2021 history. He is documented alive in 1890 (Liber 65/567) and deceased by January 1914 (Mary’s notice). A primary death notice or cemetery record would close the gap.

The two John Gilroys. The relationship between the pioneer John and the Putnam House John Gilroy — and the place of Hugh Gilroy (grantor to John in 1893, Liber 75/300) — needs to be worked out before any Gilroy deed or census entry is attributed with confidence.

The 1891 conveyance to the State of New York. A Gilroy → State of N.Y. transfer appears in the grantor index (Liber 72/291). Its parcel and purpose are not yet established; given this project’s subject matter, a possible watershed or institutional connection is worth checking.

The deeper land chain. The home lot derives from the Bailey–Brewster farm assembled in 1848–1851 (e.g., Gilbert Bailey → Walter/Samuel Brewster at Liber 21/86 and 21/98; the 1851 Brush → Brewster neighbor deed at X/120; with Brewster acquisitions reaching back to Doane and Lawrence conveyances of the 1820s–1830s). None of the reviewed deeds carries a “same premises” recital, so the chain must be reconstructed by index search rather than by citation. This is a lot-level title project in its own right and is parked for a future piece.

Map reconciliation. The 1863/1867/1890 metes-and-bounds (east side of the contemplated street; Brush north, Ganung south; the well; the right of way) should be reconciled against the Sanborn sheets to fix the home lot to a modern Eastview Avenue address.

The “first brick” quotation. The widely repeated parish line — that the Gilroys “laid the first brick” of the parish — comes specifically from Tina Grant’s June 13, 2021 anniversary article and is a modern tribute, not a historical source; it is deliberately not used as evidence here.

Village of Brewster, New York

Edward Wright’s Shop

A blacksmith shop turned tire shop, at the foot of the bank on Main Street — and the photograph its proprietor captioned himself.

Putnam County, NYFrom an 1868 roadTo the present day

Draft narrative — the chain of title for the shop lot is documented continuously from 1892 to the present, and Edward Wright’s business is documented from his own advertising, 1913–1921. The paving date that would fix the photograph, and the lot’s pre-1892 origin, remain open; unresolved questions are listed at the end.

I. The Photograph

Three men stand in front of a low, wide, board-sided building, among a scatter of automobile tires and inner tubes. One wears a workman’s long apron and rests a foot on a tire; one stands in the open doorway in overalls; the third, in suspenders, keeps to the far door. A bicycle leans against the wall at the left edge of the frame. Above the shop’s roof, on a grassy bank, stands a two-story clapboard house with a columned two-story veranda — close enough behind the shop that the two buildings share the picture the way they shared the ground.

The long sign board across the front, weathered even when the photograph was taken, reads — with the illegible spans marked:

PLUMBING  ·  EDWARD WRIGHT  ·  [&?] REPAIRING
TINSMITHING  ·  BICYCLE[S]

Painted sign board, as legible on the original print. REPAIRING resolves on the author’s scan; the short word before it, and the end of BICYCLE[S], remain weathered past certain reading.

Smaller signs fill in the trade. An Iver Johnson Motor Cycles dealer’s emblem hangs in the left window. DRY CELLS is painted beside the sill, next to a black enamel OIL plate. A paper poster near the right corner advertises bicycle and motorcycle tires. On the sill of the right window sits a large conical-lidded tin can — the tinsmith’s own product, in the tire dealer’s window.

And at the lower right, chalked or painted in white on the boarding, is a set of characters that dates the whole scene: 28 + 00. It is a highway surveyor’s station mark — a point 2,800 feet along a survey baseline — and it means that when the photographer set up, the crews laying out the paving of Main Street had already been through.

II. The Caption and Its Paper

The photograph survives with something rarer than a photograph: a typed caption by the man on the sign. On a browned scrap of paper, Edward Wright set down what the picture showed:

This picture was taken in about the year 191[?] before or about the time Main Street Brewster, N.Y. was hard surfaced. Note the Surveyors mark (28 + 00 at lower right hand corner of building. The Shop was originally Howard Smith’s Blacksmith Shop before I used it. Left to right ( EDWARD WRIGHT ) (WALTER BENEDICT ) ( FRANK L. HALL ) I operated the shop from about July 1907 to September 1920.

Typed caption signed Edward Wright, on the verso of a printed promissory-note blank.

The paper he typed it on tells its own story, and disciplines how the caption can be used. It is a blank negotiable promissory note of the Safety Vulcanizer Company of Chicago — a maker of tire-repair equipment — and its printer’s form code dates the printing to April 1926. So the caption was written at least six years after Wright left Brewster, and something like fifteen years after the shutter fell: a man in the tire trade, still, reaching for the nearest scrap of paper to label a picture of his old shop. His memory of the year is hedged in his own typing. His dates are testimony, not record — and this study treats them that way, checking each one against paper he never saw.

The checks, as the sections below lay out, come back remarkably well. The station mark he pointed to is exactly where he said. The blacksmith he named turns out to hold the lot in the deed record, in the very years his caption implies. And his most careful phrase — “before I used it,” not owned it — turns out to be the most precise thing anyone wrote about this property in a century.

The print itself has a journey worth recording. Wright carried his photographs with him out of Brewster, and somewhere in the century that followed — Daytona, and wherever the family went after — this one drifted more than a thousand miles from the building it shows. In 2026 it surfaced in a thrift shop in Arkansas, where a stranger named Jeff judged it “too important to leave there,” bought it, and — when this project found him — sold it back toward Brewster with a note: “I am glad this one found its way home.” The photograph now sits a short walk from the ground it was taken on.

III. Two Roads and a Bank

The shop’s site only makes sense once you know that the two streets bounding its lot are not the same age. The lot runs through a short block, from Main Street at the bottom of a steep bank up to Oak Street at the top — and every deed in its chain, from 1892 to 1964, bounds it on the north by

the old highway leading past the residence formerly of Harvey Bailey, deceased, now called Oak Street…

Recurring boundary language, Putnam County Liber 75 of Deeds, p. 113 (1892) through Liber 683, p. 153 (1970).

Oak Street is the old road. Main Street below it is the newcomer — and a deed for the lot next door says so outright. In March 1868, John R. Bloomer sold John Newell the adjoining parcel, bounded on one side by “the old highway running past the dwelling house of Albert Brush” and on the other by “the New Highway leading from Brewsters Station” PC 48/393. The railroad had come, a new road had been cut below the old one to reach the depot, and the strip of ground between the two roads became buildable frontage. The house in the photograph stands at the top of the bank facing the old road, where houses had always faced; the shop stood at the toe of the bank on the new one, in a position that had not existed before the railroad. Oak Street carried a second name into the twentieth century — School Street, after the district schoolhouse on its north side — and a 1912 deed uses that name alone PC 104/371, while a 1920 deed for the same land hedges with “Oak Street (sometimes called School Street)” PC 117/506: the rename, caught in the act between two instruments.

Negative findings, recorded as findings: the Beers atlas of 1867 and Reed’s map of 1876 show no structure at the shop site — though small outbuildings fall below those atlases’ threshold, so absence of depiction is not proof of absence. The Sanborn fire-insurance series, which would settle the question, does not reach this stretch of Main Street until the five-sheet edition of 1910 — the earlier editions of 1887, 1893, and 1898 end before it. The building’s construction date therefore cannot be fixed from the map record.

IV. The Blacksmith in the Chain

The shop lot’s recorded history begins on December 14, 1892, when John and Julia Newell conveyed it to J. Howard Smith for $2,000, “and the buildings thereon” PC 75/113 — improved land, though the deed does not say improved with what. Smith assumed two mortgages held by the Putnam County Savings Bank, one of them dating to April 1871: debt that had been riding on this ground for twenty-one years, and that reaches back past everything else in the record.

J. Howard Smith held the lot for thirteen years. He is the “Howard Smith” of Wright’s caption — the blacksmith whose shop this was “before I used it” — and the caption’s accuracy here is worth pausing on: a surname and given name typed from memory, decades later and a thousand miles away, landing squarely on a grantee in the chain of title. On September 27, 1905, Smith and his wife Ellen sold to Lizzie O’Loughlin for $2,750 PC 117/442 — and then the deed did something this parcel’s paper does habitually: it sat unrecorded. For fifteen years.

It finally went on record on the morning of February 27, 1920, at 9:55 — three minutes before the next deed in the chain, by which Lizzie O’Loughlin conveyed the lot to her sons Thomas J. and Edward J. O’Loughlin PC 117/444. A stale deed raced onto the record to perfect the chain immediately ahead of a family transfer: ordinary conveyancing housekeeping, with one large consequence for the history. For the whole of Lizzie O’Loughlin’s ownership — 1905 to 1920 — her title was invisible in the public index. Anyone searching the record in those years would have found the lot apparently still Smith’s.

The Tenancy, Proved by Bracketing

Lizzie O’Loughlin held title from September 27, 1905 to February 26, 1920 — an interval that wholly contains Edward Wright’s stated occupancy of July 1907 to September 1920. Wright appears nowhere in the lot’s chain of title, and the grantee indexes show no acquisition by him here. He rented the shop, throughout, from Lizzie O’Loughlin. His phrase — “before I used it” — was exact.

V. “Yes, I Am Busy”

What Wright did inside the rented building, his own advertising documents season by season. In August 1913 he ran a small square in the local press with a headline that is very nearly a self-portrait:

Yes, I Am Busy — Hustling all the while. Are you one that is making me hustle? If not it is time for you to hustle awhile. Edward Wright. Vulcanizing, Plumbing, Tinning. East Main Street. Brewster, N.Y. Telephone Connection.

Advertisement, August 1, 1913.

By August 1915 the advertisement had grown a telephone number — 156-W — and a fuller trade: plumbing attended to, “Supplies for Auto, Motor Cycle and Bicycle,” “Agent for the Iver Johnson Line of Bicycles,” and “EXPERT VULCANIZING — Drive your car THIS WAY and have the same CAREFUL ATTENTION paid to your tires they receive when first made.” A third advertisement promotes the 1916 Iver Johnson Two Speed — “a great hill climber” — under the banner “Good Roads and a Good Bicycle,” and offers vulcanizing for everything down to “Baby’s Cart.” The Iver Johnson connection is on the building itself: the Fitchburg arms-and-cycle firm built motorcycles only from 1907 to 1916, and its Motor Cycles dealer emblem hangs in the shop window in the photograph.

By September 1918 Wright appears on the Standard Oil Company of New York’s published list of Brewster dealers selling SOCONY motor gasoline — alongside, intriguingly, an F. S. Hall, whose relationship to the Frank L. Hall standing at the right of the photograph is an open question. The arc across a single decade of advertising is the arc of the American roadside itself: a plumber and tinsmith, to a bicycle agent, to a motorcycle dealer, to a tire and vulcanizing man, to a gasoline dealer — all in one small rented building at the foot of a bank, in a shop where a blacksmith had shod horses.

VI. Dating the Picture

Wright remembered only “about the year 191[?].” The photograph itself narrows it. The Sanborn sheet of April 1910 letters the building “TIN SHOP & BICYCLE REPAIRING” — the trades of the sign board, in Wright’s tenure. The Iver Johnson motorcycle emblem in the window points to the agency years, and the marque left the motorcycle business after 1916. The stacked automobile casings and inner tubes, and the tire poster, fit the vulcanizing trade of the 1915 advertisement. The road in the foreground is plainly dirt, and the surveyor’s station mark on the boarding says the paving survey had just come through — which is exactly the moment Wright’s caption assigns the picture: “before or about the time Main Street… was hard surfaced.”

The working bracket is therefore c. 1910–1916, with the later half of that span the more likely on the strength of the tire stock. The one document that would collapse the bracket to a season — the contract award or completion notice for the hard-surfacing of East Main Street — has not yet been found, and is the first item on the open list below.

VII. One Week in February, 1920

The shop’s era ended, and the record of its ending is compressed into a single remarkable week. On February 26, 1920, Lizzie O’Loughlin deeded the shop lot to her two sons. The next morning her fifteen-year-old purchase deed and the new family deed were recorded three minutes apart — and that same day, February 27, the local press reported that Edward Wright, “plumber and machinist and dealer in motorcycles and bicycles, desirous of entering upon business at Daytona, Florida,” had sold his property to John Ekstrom through the agency of James Murty, for $4,500.

What Wright sold was not the shop — it was never his to sell. His deed to Ekstrom, executed April 1, 1920 PC 117/506, conveys the house he and his wife Rose had bought in 1912 from the firm of Nichols & Adams for $3,500 PC 104/371: a parcel on the north side of Oak Street — across the old highway from the top of the shop lot, a stone’s throw from his landlady’s house on the bank. The newspaper’s figures, long puzzling, resolve against the deed itself: its “175 feet on Main street” is the deed’s 176 feet on Oak, and its “150 feet on Oak street” matches the parcel’s computed diagonal east line of about 153 feet — one parcel’s two long dimensions, with the street names attached wrongly. A reporter working the clerk’s office that morning had the O’Loughlin recordings and the Wright sale in front of him at once, and blended a tenant’s departure and a landlord’s reorganization into a single paragraph. The paper record, read whole, unblends it.

Wright wound the business down through the year — his caption remembers operating until September — and the equipment was still dispersing the next winter: in February 1921, C. C. Merrick of Towners advertised that “Having bought Edward Wright’s Lowell vulcanizer I am prepared to repair tires and tubes.” Then Brewster loses him to Daytona — where, to judge from the Chicago vulcanizer-company form he typed his caption on years later, he stayed in the tire trade.

VIII. The Lot Afterward

The O’Loughlin family kept the lot for forty-four years. Edward J. O’Loughlin took his wife Anna into a tenancy by the entirety in 1937 PC 223/396; by 1964, conveying alone from Tonetta Lake Road, he had outlived her, and he sold to Frank J. and Gail L. Manente, who were already living on Main Street a few doors away PC 603/487. The Manente years produced the survey whose courses — drill hole on the north side of Main, 80.54 feet along the street, up the bank 133 feet, 78.64 feet along Oak — every deed since has copied, down to a misspelled neighbor’s name frozen in the boilerplate. Through the Roberto PC 927/42, Scomillio, and Lugo ownerships to the present, the parcel has kept exactly the shape it had when the blacksmith worked at the bottom of it: one through lot, house above, shop ground below, bounded by the same neighbors the 1892 deed named.

The shop building itself is gone — when, the record has not yet said. The ground where it stood is a graveled parking apron at the foot of the bank. The bank above is terraced in dry-laid stone; the upper tiers, with their timber stairs and stockade fencing, are modern work, and only the lowest course, immediately behind the shop’s footprint, could be contemporary with the building. The house still stands at the top, facing the old road, as it did over the shop’s roof in the photograph.

One recorded encumbrance rides beneath the whole chain: mining rights reserved to others, recited in every deed from 1892 through 1970 — the routine fossil of Brewster’s iron-district past. A utility easement of 2007 PC 1817/425 is the only modern addition.

Open Research
  • The hard-surfacing of East Main Street: petition, contract, completion — the single document that would date the photograph to a season. Highway survey plans carrying the 28+00 stationing would also fix the building’s footprint to the foot.
  • The lot’s pre-1892 origin. No recorded conveyance to John Newell for this parcel has been found in the grantee index, 1812–1892 — a genuine negative, since the county’s online index is complete from formation. The 1871 Putnam County Savings Bank mortgage, whose mortgagor’s name would settle the question, is the decisive unpulled instrument.
  • When the shop building was erected, and when it came down.
  • Walter Benedict and Frank L. Hall, the other two men in the photograph — and whether Frank L. Hall is the F. S. Hall of the 1918 SOCONY dealer list.
  • The 1905–1907 interval: who used the shop between Smith’s sale and Wright’s arrival.
  • Thomas J. O’Loughlin’s half-interest, held from 1920 and absent by 1937.
  • Edward and Rose Wright in Daytona: the 1930 census, Florida directories, and the rest of the tire man’s story.
  • The exact placement of Wright’s own former house lot on the modern tax map, via the Ekstrom chain forward from 1920.
